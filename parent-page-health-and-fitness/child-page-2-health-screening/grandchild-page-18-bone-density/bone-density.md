The same DEXA scan that measures fat and muscle also measures how dense my bones are. This is the quiet good-news story of the series: my bones were already in good shape in April, and October didn't change that. October's number also looks like a big jump, but I'm not reading much into it, and the scan report itself says why.

## What it is

**BMD (bone mineral density)** is how much mineral is packed into a given area of bone. More is better. Low BMD means weaker bones and a higher risk of fractures.

The scan reports two comparison scores:

- **T-score** compares you to a healthy young adult at peak bone mass. This is the one used to diagnose osteoporosis, mainly after menopause or in men over 50. −1 and above is normal.
- **Z-score** compares you to people of your own age and sex. This is the one that matters for someone my age. A Z-score of −2.0 or lower is "below the expected range for age" and worth investigating. Anything above that is fine.

## What I'm aiming for

- **Z-scores comfortably above −2.0** at every site, ideally above 0.
- **No drop over time.** Peak bone mass is reached around your late 20s to 30s, so the job now is to hold onto it.

## Was April a problem?

No. April was the full bone scan (spine, hip and whole body), and the radiologist's report said all my Z-scores were within the expected range, the WHO classification was normal, and fracture risk wasn't increased. My spine was above average for my age (Z-score +1.5). The hip was close to average (−0.2), which is still well within normal.

## October: higher, but don't over-read it

October's scan was whole-body only (no separate spine or hip scans), and the whole-body BMD came back 0.059 g/cm² higher, a 4.9% increase. On paper that's a big jump, well above the 0.014 g/cm² the scan counts as a real change.

But look at the **#** next to the change in the report snippet above. It means **"dissimilar scan types or analysis methods"**. The two scans weren't set up the same way, so the machine itself is saying it can't fairly compare them. Bone also changes slowly, and a real 5% gain in six months would be unusual for an adult.

What I take from it: **my bones are still normal to good, and nothing went backwards** during a big cut, which is the main thing I'd want to know.

What probably helps my bones generally:

- **Load.** BJJ and strength training put force through the bones, which is the main signal for them to stay dense.
- **Vitamin D**, which went from insufficient to desirable over the same period (see [Vitamin D](/health-and-fitness/health-screening/vitamin-d)).
- **Enough protein and calcium.** My blood calcium was normal both times.

## What's next

- No action needed. Keep lifting and grappling once my injuries allow.
- Being very lean for a long time can be hard on bones, which is another reason not to chase lower body fat (see [Body Fat](/health-and-fitness/health-screening/body-fat)).
- If I scan again, I'll ask for the same scan type as last time so the numbers are comparable.
